2. Actual vs the Expected plan

The plan was written before the backend existed and frozen. Three things were wrong, and they were wrong in instructive ways.

What held

  • Every first call. Inventory on 101, Facilities on 102, Inventory on 103 — including the reasoning. On 101 Inventory goes first because if stock were non-zero the ticket closes in one step with no payment at all.
  • Every exclusion. Facilities absent from 101 and 103; Inventory and Customer Service absent from 102.
  • 102 the shortest run, as predicted.
  • The negative tool predictions. No get_rent_status on 101, no payment tool on 103, no create_purchase_order succeeding anywhere.
  • The substantive findings — 0 on hand short 1, invoice 3 days overdue, no delivery date, 8 of 20 short 12, sizes not fungible, margin in dollars, no invented vendor.

Miss 1 — lateral delegation happened in the wrong place

I predicted Inventory → Accounting on 101 and Facilities → Accounting on 102, reasoning that the specialist who discovers a money problem should hand it over directly. Both went through the Boss instead. And on 103, where I explicitly argued a lateral hop would add a round trip without adding information, the run's only lateral hand-off occurred: Inventory → Accounting.

Exactly inverted. The lesson: with a hub agent holding the ticket context, agents route back to the hub by default. Full connectivity is a capability, not a behaviour — you get hub-and-spoke unless the prompt makes the lateral case specific, and even then the model may apply it elsewhere.

Miss 2 — no customer drafts

I predicted Customer Service as the last hop on 101 and 103 and put the draft content in the plan. It never ran. Of the three misses this is the one that cost the shop something real.

Miss 3 — 103 raised an approval after all

I predicted "probably no approval — no money needs to move for a discount," which was right about the cash and wrong about the conclusion. Granting margin away is exactly a human's call. The plan was written before request_decision_approval existed; the gap only became visible when a human looked at ticket 103 and found nothing to approve. The plan was reasoning correctly from an incomplete tool set — which is the most useful kind of wrong prediction, because it pointed at a missing tool rather than a bad agent.

3. What would have been simpler as one agent with tools?

All three tickets would have been simpler with one agent. Every run was a single ticket handled sequentially, with no two agents ever working at the same time. One agent holding all 16 tools would have produced the same decisions with fewer tokens and no delegation machinery.

The concrete cost of five agents here

TicketMulti-agentOne agent, same tools
1022 delegations, 5 tool calls, 4 model round trips for hand-offsget_ticket → get_rent_status → get_cash_balance → request_payment_approval. Four calls, no hand-offs.
1012 delegations, 7 tool callsSix calls in one context — and the invoice is already visible in get_ticket's linked rows, so nothing needed to be "discovered" by a second agent.
1033 delegations, 11 tool calls (+9 cached)Stock and margin are independent lookups. The 9 cached calls exist because three agents re-asked the same questions.

Every delegation is a full model round trip: the question is written, the sub-agent re-reads the ticket, answers in structured form, and the hub re-reads that answer. The per-run tool cache I had to add is itself evidence — it exists purely to absorb three agents asking the same question.

What the team structure did buy, even at this size

  • Permission scoping. Customer Service physically cannot quote a price, and Facilities cannot prepare a payment. With one agent, "do not promise a discount" is one line in a long prompt competing with fifty others. The disclosure bug I caught early — a draft telling a customer the shop owed its printer $840 — was fixed by editing one small prompt, not by re-balancing a monolith.
  • Attribution. The audit trail says Accounting called get_cash_balance 31 times. A single agent would have shown 31 anonymous calls, and the loop would have been far harder to localise.
  • Prompt modularity. Five prompts of ~500 words each, edited independently. The discount fix touched boss.md and accounting.md and could not regress Inventory.

Where the team would actually start to pay for itself

  • Breadth. Twenty tickets of eight types. One prompt covering all of it becomes unmaintainable long before five do.
  • Parallelism. Nothing here ran concurrently. Fifty tickets with independent stock and cash questions could.
  • Different trust levels. If Customer Service ever gained a real send capability, having it in a separate agent with a separate tool set stops being tidiness and becomes the security boundary.

One more piece of evidence from the Cash tab: the entire financial output of three runs and five agents is two payment rows — $840.00 and $2,400.00 — plus one discount that moved nothing. The decision surface was small; the machinery around it was not. For this homework the five-agent design is the subject, not the optimum. The defensible claim is that it cost roughly 2–3× the tokens of a single agent and bought scoping, attribution and modularity that paid off three times during development.

5. Three problems the agent team could not solve — and what it would take

Each gap is one this run actually exposed, not a hypothetical.

a. A customer returns a hoodie and wants a refund

Why it fails now. The Cash tab shows every movement this shop can make: two rows out, nothing in. Money leaves only through pay_approved, which is hard-wired to settle an invoice (ref_id 501) or a lease (ref_id 1) — the only two kinds on that tab. There is no way to record a sale, so there is nothing to refund against: no sales table, no order ids, no concept of a return. Inventory has no way to put a unit back on the shelf — check_stock reads but never writes qty. An agent asked to handle a refund today would correctly report that it cannot.

What it needs. Tables for sales (order id, SKU, size, qty, price paid, date) and returns. Tools: lookup_sale(order_id), receive_return (incrementing inventory and writing a return row), and issue_refund — gated exactly like pay_approved, with a new rule that a refund may never exceed the price actually paid. A Returns agent would own condition and eligibility (within the window? resaleable?), delegating the money to Accounting and the reply to Customer Service. Accounting's "cash only goes out" assumption would also need revisiting, since a refund is cash out against revenue that was never modelled.

b. "Which sizes should we reorder before the season starts?"

Why it fails now. The database is a single snapshot. Ticket 103 is the proof: the team established 8 on hand against 20 requested, but nothing on the 103 tab says whether 8 is a week of sales or a year of them, so "short 12" could not become "reorder this much, this often". check_stock answers "how many are there", never "how fast do they go". An agent asked this today can only count current stock — and under the no-invented-data rule it should refuse to forecast, which is correct and useless.

What it needs. A dated sales history, plus sales_velocity(sku, size, window_days) and reorder_point(sku, size) combining velocity with the vendor's lead_days — the one input the shop already has. A Demand or Planning agent would own the forecast, because the failure mode is a confident number with no data behind it, and that deserves its own prompt with explicit refusal conditions ("fewer than N weeks of history, say so"). Inventory would consume its output rather than compute it.

c. Actually telling the customer and the vendor

Why it fails now. This is the gap the run made most visible. Tickets 101 and 103 both closed with a named requester — Tauhid Zaman and the Yale AI Club — told nothing, and even if Customer Service had drafted a reply it could not have gone out: by design there is no send capability anywhere, not disabled but absent. Drafts sit in the drafts table and a human must act. Same for vendors: create_purchase_order writes a row and says plainly "no vendor has been contacted". So the shop can decide everything and communicate nothing. In this homework that is the right trade; in a real shop it is a ticket that never closes.

What it needs. An email or messaging integration behind a send_draft(draft_id, approved_by) tool, gated the same way money is: a human approves the specific draft, the tool refuses anything not explicitly approved, and it records what was sent, to whom and when, because a sent message cannot be recalled. A Communications agent should own the outbound channel rather than Customer Service, so that "write it" and "send it" stay in different hands — the same separation that keeps Accounting from approving its own payments. Vendor orders would need a parallel transmit_purchase_order and an order_status read, since a PO the vendor never received is worse than no PO at all.

They fail because the tool layer cannot represent them: no revenue, no history, no outbound channel. That is the real lesson of the build — the agents were never the binding constraint. Every serious gap I hit was a missing tool or a missing table, and every important guarantee came from a tool that refused rather than a prompt that asked nicely.
